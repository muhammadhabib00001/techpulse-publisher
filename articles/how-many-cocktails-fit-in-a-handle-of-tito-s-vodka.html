How Many Cocktails Fit in a Handle of Tito's Vodka?

Find out exactly how many cocktails fit in a 1.75-liter handle of Tito's Handmade Vodka, with real pour math, party planning guides, and recipe costs.

By Julia Vance · November 19, 2026 · 6 min read
✓ Fact Checked · Verified Sources

Quick Answer: A standard 1.75-liter handle contains 59.2 fluid ounces, yielding exactly 39 standard cocktails poured at 1.5 ounces each, or 29 stiffer drinks poured at 2 ounces. Accounting for minor bar spills and heavy free-pouring, practical hosts should plan on getting 35 to 37 mixed drinks per handle.

Calculating inventory before throwing a party is the difference between a smooth evening and a chaotic midnight run to the nearest retailer. If you have have you asked How Many Cocktails Actually Fit in a Handle of Tito's, the true figure comes down to arithmetic and pour discipline. A standard bottle referred to colloquially as a "handle" contains 1.75 liters of spirit, which equals roughly 59.2 fluid ounces. When measured with a jigger, that volume supplies enough liquor for dozens of drinks, but real-world conditions often shift those theoretical totals.

Produced in Austin, Texas, Tito's Handmade Vodka is distilled from yellow corn in pot stills, yielding a gluten-free spirit that dominates American retail shelves. Because of its neutral flavor profile and mid-tier price point, it serves as the default base for highballs, mules, and martinis in homes across the country. Understanding how far one large glass jug stretches lets you balance your beverage budget without leaving your guests dry before dinner ends.

Planning a party budget often overlaps with general social entertainment planning, where getting your supply ratios right prevents mid-evening runs to the corner liquor store. Whether you are running a casual backyard barbecue or preparing a formal wedding reception bar, measuring pours precisely helps track both your alcohol distribution and overall cost per serving.

The Mathematics of a 1.75-Liter Handle

Liquor packaging follows metric standards set by federal trade regulations, even in regions that otherwise rely on the imperial system. A 1.75-liter container holds 1,750 milliliters of liquid. Converting milliliters to standard United States fluid ounces requires dividing by 29.5735. This conversion establishes that a full handle holds precisely 59.174 fluid ounces of spirit. Knowing this baseline number lets you calculate drink counts across any cocktail recipe by simply dividing the bottle's capacity by the target pour size.

Fluid Ounces and Milliliters Explained

Many home hosts mix up fluid ounces with dry ounces, leading to inaccurate drink batching. Liquid measuring tools mark fluid ounces (fl oz), where one US fluid ounce equals roughly 30 milliliters. When bartenders measure a standard single pour, they target 1.5 fluid ounces (44.36 milliliters). Dividing 59.174 ounces by 1.5 gives exactly 39.45 portions. If every drink is poured strictly using a standard jigger, a single handle delivers 39 complete drinks with nearly half an ounce left at the bottom of the bottle.

Standard 1.5-Ounce Versus 2-Ounce Pours

Drink style drastically changes bottle longevity. A basic vodka soda or screwdriver usually takes a 1.5-ounce shot, yielding the textbook 39 drinks. However, spirit-forward stirred drinks like a classic dry vodka martini or an espresso martini regularly call for 2 full ounces of vodka. If your event centers on 2-ounce pours, dividing 59.174 by 2 yields 29.58 servings. That means you drop from almost 40 drinks down to 29 full drinks per handle simply by increasing the base pour by half an ounce.

Factoring in Spillage, Over-Pouring, and Waste

Real parties never run with commercial restaurant efficiency unless a professional bartender oversees the bar. Unmeasured "free-pouring" from an unassisted handle, especially one without a speed pour spout, frequently leads to 2.25-ounce or 2.5-ounce dumps into tall glasses filled with ice. In addition, small spills, cocktail shaker wash line residue, and ice dilution mistakes claim roughly 5% to 8% of the bottle. In unmonitored self-serve environments, hosts should plan on getting 32 to 35 drinks out of a handle rather than the theoretical 39.

Drink Yields Across Popular Tito's Cocktails

Cocktails vary widely in volume, balance, and liquor requirements. A simple two-ingredient highball uses a modest portion of spirit stretched over bubbly seltzer, while sour builds and specialty cordials demand strict ratios to remain drinkable. Knowing the exact vodka requirement for each signature drink recipe guarantees you buy the correct quantity of mixers along with your spirits.

Highballs: Tito's and Soda, Tonic, or Cranberry

The staple highball combines vodka with carbonated water, flavored tonic, or fruit juice in a tall Collins glass packed with cubed ice. The baseline recipe requires 1.5 ounces of vodka and 4 to 5 ounces of mixer. A 1.75-liter handle produces roughly 39 highballs. To support this volume, you will need approximately 160 to 195 ounces of mixer, which equates to about three 2-liter soda bottles or roughly seven 1-liter club soda bottles per handle of vodka opened.

Moscow Mules and Copper Mug Builds

The Moscow Mule remains one of the most requested vodka cocktails in home settings. The traditional formula pairs 2.0 ounces of vodka with 0.5 ounces of freshly squeezed lime juice and 4 ounces of spicy ginger beer, served over crushed ice in an insulated copper mug. Because the vodka pour sits at a full 2 ounces, a single handle produces roughly 29 mules. For every handle dedicated to mules, prepare to stock fifteen 12-ounce cans of ginger beer and at least 15 whole limes for juice and garnishes.

Vodka Martinis and Stiff Stirred Classics

Martinis leave no room for weak measurements because the alcohol provides both the texture and the flavor foundation. A standard vodka martini pairs 2.5 ounces of vodka with 0.5 ounces of dry vermouth, stirred with cracked ice and strained into a chilled glass. At 2.5 ounces per drink, a 1.75-liter handle yields only 23 cocktails. Parties highlighting classic martinis burn through liquor stock nearly 40% faster than events serving tall highballs, meaning hosts must adjust their shopping lists accordingly.

Party Planning Math and Cost Breakdown

Hosting friends requires balanced hospitality, blending casual party games and media entertainment with dependable bar prep that keeps everyone refreshed. Budgeting for alcohol requires looking at unit economics rather than just the register price. A 1.75-liter bottle represents significant savings over purchasing standard 750-milliliter fifths, lowering the average cost per pour across an entire evening.

Cost Per Drink at Current Retail Pricing

In most American states, a 1.75-liter handle of Tito's retails between $31.99 and $36.99 depending on local excise taxes and retail competition. Taking a standard price of $33.99, let us evaluate the drink economics. For a standard 1.5-ounce pour delivering 39 drinks per bottle, the liquor cost works out to approximately $0.87 per serving. If you prepare 2-ounce pours delivering 29 drinks, your liquor cost rises to roughly $1.17 per drink. Compared to buying four separate 750ml bottles at $21.99 each ($87.96 total for 3 liters), handles cut your liquor expenditure by nearly 30% per ounce.

Calculating Bottle Counts for Gatherings

Event planners rely on a proven rule of thumb: expect each drinking guest to consume two drinks during the first hour and one drink for every subsequent hour of the event. For a four-hour gathering with 20 vodka-drinking guests, the group will consume roughly 100 drinks (20 guests multiplied by 5 drinks each). Dividing 100 drinks by 35 realistic pours per handle shows you need 2.85 bottles. In this scenario, purchasing three handles covers the crowd with a safe margin for error, accounting for generous pours and dropped glasses.

Batching Punches and Pitchers Efficiently

If you prefer mingling over standing behind a counter making individual drinks all night, large-format batched punches offer the ideal solution. A standard 2-gallon punch bowl comfortably accepts an entire 1.75-liter handle alongside complementary juices and sodas. A classic crowd-pleasing punch formula combines one handle of vodka, 64 ounces of cranberry juice, 32 ounces of pineapple juice, and 48 ounces of ginger ale or lemon-lime soda. This recipe yields roughly 50 four-ounce punch servings poured over ice, distributing the alcohol evenly without requiring any measuring tools during the party.

Final Thoughts

Key Takeaway: A 1.75-liter handle of Tito's holds 59.2 ounces, providing 39 standard 1.5-ounce cocktails or 29 heavier 2-ounce drinks. In real-world party environments with free-pouring and minor waste, expect an average return of 35 servings per bottle, costing under ninety cents per drink at standard retail rates.

Mastering home bartending math removes the guesswork from stocking your home bar cart or planning an expansive weekend reception. By using dedicated measuring jiggers, investing in inexpensive bottle pour spouts, and keeping appropriate ratios of ice and mixers on hand, you ensure every ounce of your handle goes directly into enjoyable, well-balanced drinks for your guests.

Frequently Asked Questions

Frequently Asked Questions

How many shots are in a handle of Tito's compared to a fifth?

A 1.75-liter handle contains 39 standard 1.5-ounce shots, whereas a standard 750-milliliter fifth contains approximately 16.9 shots. Buying one handle gives you more alcohol than two full fifth bottles combined, providing 2.33 times the volume for a lower total price.

How many handles of Tito's do I need for a party of 50 people?

For a four-hour gathering of 50 guests where vodka is the sole liquor, plan for roughly 250 total drinks based on five drinks per person. Assuming 35 realistic mixed drinks per handle, you would need seven handles. If serving beer and wine alongside vodka, two to three handles will be plenty.

Does Tito's vodka go bad after opening a 1.75-liter handle?

Tito's does not expire or spoil after opening because its 40% alcohol by volume (80 proof) creates an environment where bacteria and mold cannot survive. However, an opened bottle kept for more than two years with significant headspace may experience minor flavor changes due to slow oxidation.
